# Musical ideas, phrases, and development

Use these principles across musical styles. Begin with the feature the brief makes
important: a melody, rhythm, harmony, timbre, or dramatic event. There is no required
composition order. Ask what the listener should notice; make that role clear.
Genre conventions belong to the user's brief, not to a built-in recipe.

## A recognizable melodic identity

For “include a memorable melody,” establish a compact identity in rhythm and
contour. State it clearly, repeat it, and vary a meaningful detail. Shape phrases
toward an arrival and leave space for an answer. Compare the theme alone with its
arrangement: an obscured idea may need less accompaniment rather than new notes.
No checklist, scale, or note count guarantees memorability.

Original example: pitches D4 F4 A4 G4 F4 E4 (MIDI 62, 65, 69, 67, 65, 64),
starts 0, 0.5, 1.5, 2, 3, 3.5, and lengths 0.4, 0.8, 0.4, 0.8, 0.4, 0.4
in quarter-note beats. The short-long-short opening and rising-then-falling
contour form an identity. Create a four-beat `theme` pattern; repeat it unchanged,
then create an `answer` pattern ending E4 D4 with a longer final D. The exercise
illustrates a technique, not an ideal melody or a preferred style.

## Form and harmonic movement

A sentence can present an idea twice and develop a fragment toward a cadence.
A period can pair a less conclusive phrase with a more conclusive answer. These
are useful tonal models, not mandatory eight-bar templates. Asymmetric phrases,
drones, open loops, and continuous development serve other intentions.

For “make the return feel earned,” establish an idea, redirect or reduce it in a
contrasting passage, then recover recognizable material. Name sections and their
purpose before filling every track. Change harmonic rhythm, texture, register,
or phrase ending deliberately; not every contrast needs an entirely new theme.

Original tonal exercise: D minor → B-flat → G minor → A → D minor. Retain common
upper notes where useful and let C-sharp approach D at the ending. A stepwise or
inverted bass may connect chords better than roots. Avoiding parallel fifths is
relevant to some independent-voice traditions, while parallel harmony is useful
elsewhere. Fixed phrase lengths, root bass, and one chord per bar are choices.

## Rhythm, expression, and revision

Establish which events carry the pulse and which create contrast. Repetition can
support recognition; changes in accents, rests, durations, or placement can keep
it responsive. Swing deliberately changes subdivision proportions. Humanization
should follow phrasing and role, rather than apply noise to every note.
Both legacy MIDI and arrangements support fractional beats; export rounds to
the MIDI tick resolution. Fine timing is not restricted to a coarse beat grid.

- Aimless: retain rhythmic identity, reduce unrelated changes, choose a destination.
- Static: keep the recognizable opening, alter its answer or surrounding texture.
- Crowded: reduce competing activity before adding volume or more doubling.
- Mechanical: shape accents, lengths, rests, and expression across a phrase.
- Wrong feeling: compare tempo, register, density, articulation, and context.
  Major/minor or genre labels do not determine a listener's emotional response.

Clone a shared pattern before changing just one occurrence. Use revision-checked
targeted edits and preserve unrelated IDs. Report what changed and why; listener
acceptance remains separate from technical validation.

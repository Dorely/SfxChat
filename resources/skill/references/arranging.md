# Instrumental roles, register, and texture

Use these principles for any collection of instruments or synthesized layers.
Identify foreground, support, bass, pulse, and accents before adding tracks.
One part may change roles between sections. Doubling can emphasize an arrival;
continuous doubling may erase contrast. Dense writing can be intentional, but
equal activity and equal volume in every part often hide the intended focus.

For “make the tune clearer without changing it,” an original revision keeps the
theme pattern untouched, lowers the supporting pad from gain 0.5 to 0.32, reduces
its note count, and moves its busiest passage into a gap in the melody. Render
initial and revised versions. If clarity improves only because the whole mix is
louder, compare again with matched preview levels. These are listening hypotheses.

Register affects both spectrum and perceived role. Wider low spacing can help
separate bass and harmony; close upper intervals can add color. There is no fixed
octave assigned to every melody or every bass. For acoustic imitations consider
breathing, bow changes, playable range, and articulation. A sustained sample does
not establish what a real player can sustain. Vienna's violin discussion shows
why string and technique matter as well as pitch; flute projection also varies
with register. Reduce accompaniment when a delicate source needs room.

SoundFont controls are limited by its actual samples. Expression and note lengths
can shape phrases; they do not select recorded legato or spiccato transitions.
Use `local_catalog` and the bank's preset list, not imagined articulations. A role
that needs a synthetic transformation may be better served by synthesis or sampling.

Failure symptoms: a buried lead calls for less competition; an unchanging wall of
sound calls for role exchange or subtraction; breathless lines need intentional
rests; disconnected layers may need shared phrasing rather than more reverb.
Sometimes those effects are intentional. Follow the brief and compare alternatives.
